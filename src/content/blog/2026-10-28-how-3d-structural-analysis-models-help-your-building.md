Before a single column is cast on site, a building can already be built once on a computer. A **3-D structural analysis model** is that first, virtual version: every column, beam, slab and support is drawn as part of one connected frame, loads are applied, and the engineer checks how the structure responds. This guide explains what such a model is, how to read the pictures engineers produce, and why it matters for the building you pay for.

## What a structural analysis model is

A structural analysis model is a simplified, line-and-surface version of the building. Columns and beams are represented by lines with a known cross-section, slabs and walls by surfaces, and the base of the building by supports. The model is not a picture of the finished house. It is a calculation tool that lets an engineer see how weight and forces travel through the structure.

The workflow is straightforward: the architectural drawings give the layout, the engineer builds the model, loads are applied, the results are checked, and the final structural drawings are prepared from the checked design. Our guide to [understanding structural drawings for buildings](/blog/understanding-structural-drawings-for-buildings) explains the drawings that come out at the end of that process.

## How to read the model pictures

Software uses colours and symbols so that the parts of the frame are easy to tell apart. The exact colours depend on the program and settings, but in the images below you can see a few common conventions:

- **Columns** drawn as vertical members, often in a distinct colour such as green or blue
- **Beams** drawn as horizontal members, in yellow or red in these pictures
- **Slabs and floors** shown as transparent or shaded surfaces between the beams
- **Supports** shown as small triangles or markers at the base, where the frame is held
- **Special elements** such as stair cores, lift shafts and roof-level service blocks, often shown in a different colour

## A multi-storey frame with slabs

This model shows a regular frame with blue columns, red beams and floor slabs between them. Regular layouts like this are efficient because columns repeat at similar spacing, which keeps beam sizes manageable and makes the structure easier to build.

## A ten-storey frame

Here the model shows a taller frame with service blocks at roof level. As a building gets taller, the columns at the lower floors carry more weight, and wind or earthquake forces matter more. These are the things the engineer checks in the model before sizes are fixed on the drawings.

![3-D structural analysis model of a ten-storey building frame](/blog/structural-model-ten-storey-frame.webp)

## A low-rise frame with stair cores

Not every building is tall. This model shows a low-rise frame with stair cores. Even for a smaller building, modelling the stairs and openings helps to make sure the slabs and beams around them are properly supported.

## A model with support restraints

The last model shows a structural element with its supports clearly marked at the base. Support conditions are among the most important inputs to a model, because they describe how the structure is held in place. A wrong assumption about a support can change the results completely, which is why engineers state these assumptions clearly.

## Why a model matters to you as an owner

You do not need to read the model yourself, but it benefits your project in practical ways:

1. **Safety checks before construction.** Problems are found on a computer, where they are cheap to fix, rather than on site.
2. **Right-sized members.** Columns and beams are sized for the loads they carry, which avoids both under-design and unnecessary extra material.
3. **Better coordination.** The same layout feeds the structural drawings, and later the estimate and the BOQ.
4. **Clear communication.** A picture of the frame helps owners understand how their building stands up.

A model is only as good as its inputs. It depends on correct loads, realistic support conditions and reliable information about the soil. That is why we recommend a soil investigation for larger buildings, and why the structural design should always be checked by a qualified engineer.

## Questions to ask your engineer

- What loads and soil assumptions were used in the model?
- Were stairs, lift shafts and openings included?
- How do the drawings show the checked results?
- Who checked the design before drawings were issued?
