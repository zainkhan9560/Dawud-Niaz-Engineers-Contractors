When you start planning a house, one of the first questions is whether to build only the **grey structure** or to go for **complete construction**. The two terms are used everywhere, but they are not always understood in the same way, and that gap in understanding is behind many disagreements between owners and contractors. This guide explains what each term usually means, where the line between them sits, and how to choose.

The picture at the top shows a house at exactly this stage. The brick walls are up on two floors, concrete lintels span the window and door openings, and the openings themselves are still empty. There is no plaster, flooring, wiring finish or paint yet, and timber props and poles are still on site for supporting formwork. This is what a grey structure looks like.

## What a grey structure looks like on site

It helps to know what you are looking at when you visit a house at this stage. Using the picture at the top as a guide, check the following:

- **Walls.** Brick courses should be level and plumb, with joints filled and the wall thickness matching the drawings.
- **Openings.** Door and window openings should be in the positions and sizes shown on the plan, with a concrete lintel or beam above each one.
- **Projections.** Concrete ledges or sunshades over openings should be properly supported and match the drawings.
- **Bare brickwork and exposed concrete.** Both are unfinished at this stage, so ask how they will be protected from rain until plastering.
- **Props and formwork.** Timber or steel props should stay in place for as long as your engineer specifies, so that new concrete is not loaded too early.

A grey structure is not a finished house, but it is the best moment to catch mistakes in layout or openings, before plaster and fittings cover them.

## What grey structure usually includes

Grey structure is the stage where the building exists as a structure but is not yet finished or ready to live in. It generally covers the structural and masonry work up to the point just before plaster finishes and fittings. A typical grey structure scope includes:

- Site clearing, marking and excavation
- Foundations and plinth beam work
- Columns, beams and roof or floor slabs in reinforced concrete
- Brick or block masonry for walls
- Staircase structure
- Basic provision for sleeves and conduits that must be placed before concrete is cast

Because the exact list changes from one contractor to another, the safest approach is to get the grey structure scope written down in detail. ## What complete construction adds

Complete construction takes the same building through to a usable, finished state. On top of the grey structure, it normally adds:

- Plaster on internal and external walls
- Electrical wiring, switches and distribution board
- Plumbing, drainage and sanitary fittings
- Flooring and wall tiles
- Doors, windows and woodwork
- Paint and surface finishing
- External works such as boundary walls, gates and paving, if they are in scope

Finishing items are where the biggest variation in cost and quality appears. Two houses with identical grey structures can end up with very different totals once tiles, fixtures, joinery and lighting are chosen. That is why it helps to decide the level of finishing early, even if you plan to complete the house in stages.

## Why many owners start with grey structure

Building the grey structure first is a common and sensible approach, especially for owners who:

1. **Want to spread spending over time.** The structure is the largest single block of work and can be completed first, with finishing done as funds become available.
2. **Want more control over finishes.** Some owners prefer to buy tiles, fittings and woodwork themselves or appoint separate specialists.
3. **Are still deciding on interior details.** Seeing the actual rooms and spaces helps owners make better choices about layouts, lighting and finishes.
4. **Want to compare contractors.** Some owners use different contractors for structure and finishing.

The structure is also the part where mistakes are hardest and most expensive to correct later, so quality of design and supervision matter a great deal at this stage. ## Why some owners choose complete construction

Complete construction, or a full turnkey arrangement, suits owners who prefer a single point of responsibility. One team plans, builds and finishes the house, so there are fewer handovers between different parties. Benefits include:

- **One contract and one schedule,** which makes it easier to see when the house will be ready.
- **Better coordination between trades.** Electrical, plumbing and finishing work are planned together with the structure, which reduces rework such as breaking finished walls to run pipes.
- **Clearer accountability.** If something is wrong, there is one team responsible for putting it right.

## Points to settle before you choose

Whichever route you take, a few decisions should be settled first.

### The exact scope in writing

Ask for a written list of what is included and excluded. Items that are often disputed include excavation and backfilling, soil filling, water tank, septic tank, boundary wall, staircase finishing, and electrical or plumbing sleeves. A clear scope prevents arguments later.

### The drawings

Both routes depend on good drawings. Architectural, structural and services drawings should be prepared and checked before the first excavation. ### The budget for both stages

If you build grey structure first, you should still prepare an estimate for the finishing stage. Otherwise the project can stall when funds run short, and an unfinished building left exposed to weather can deteriorate. A [construction cost estimate](/services/construction-cost-estimation-lahore) for the full house, with the structure and finishing shown separately, gives you a realistic picture from the start. ### Planning for services

Even if you finish later, the layout of electrical conduits, plumbing lines, drainage and any future additions should be planned during the structural stage. Adding provisions later is usually harder and messier than planning them from the start.

## Questions to ask your contractor

- What exactly is included in the grey structure price?
- Which materials and brands are assumed for cement, steel and bricks?
- How will quality be checked at each stage, such as steel placement before concrete is poured?
- How will variations to the scope be priced and approved?
- If I plan to finish later, what protection will be given to the structure in the meantime?

## Which one should you choose?

There is no single right answer. Grey structure first can suit owners who need flexibility with budget and timing. Complete construction can suit owners who value convenience, coordination and a single team accountable for the result. The best choice is the one that fits your finances, your timeline and how involved you want to be.
