Structural drawings describe the skeleton of a building: the parts that carry its weight and keep it standing. They are different from architectural drawings, which focus on layout and appearance. A safe, well-built building needs both, and they must agree with each other.

The picture at the top of this article is a simplified structural layout plan. Grid lines labelled A to D and 1 to 4 locate the building, a footing sits under every column, and beams join the columns in both directions. Real drawings add sizes, levels and steel details to this layout, but the basic idea is the same: every column must stand on a footing, and every floor must be tied to the foundation through a continuous path for loads.

## What structural drawings usually show

For a reinforced concrete building, a structural drawing set commonly covers:

- **Foundations:** the size, depth and reinforcement of the footings that spread the building's load into the ground.
- **Columns:** their positions, sizes and steel reinforcement.
- **Beams:** the horizontal members that carry loads from slabs to columns.
- **Slabs:** the floors and roof, with thickness and reinforcement layout.
- **Stairs and other elements:** such as lintels, retaining walls or water tanks.
- **Notes and schedules:** material grades and bar details for the builder to follow.

## Why they depend on the architectural plan

Column and beam positions must fit the room layout. A column placed in the middle of a planned doorway, or a beam that lowers a ceiling too much, is a design conflict. Coordinating the two sets of drawings early avoids expensive changes later.

## Why the ground matters

The type of soil and the conditions on your plot affect the foundation design. Ask your engineer what information they need about the site, and follow their advice about site investigation where it is recommended.

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Starting work without structural drawings.** Rules of thumb are not a substitute for a design.
2. **Changing the plan after the structure is designed.** Adding a floor or removing a wall can change the loads. Ask the engineer before you change anything.
3. **Ignoring the drawings on site.** The builder should work from the latest approved set, and someone should check the work against it.
4. **Using different materials than specified.** The design assumes particular steel and concrete strengths.

## Supervision matters too

Good drawings only help if they are followed. Regular checking of reinforcement, formwork and concrete work by a qualified person helps to catch mistakes while they can still be fixed.
