A warehouse can look like a simple shed, but a good one is the result of many early decisions. The way you use the building decides its size, its floor strength, its doors and its structure. Settling those decisions before design begins saves money, avoids redesign and keeps the project on schedule. This guide covers the main points.

The picture at the top is a simplified drawing of a steel portal frame, a common structure for warehouses. Columns and rafters form repeating frames, purlins join the frames and carry the roof sheets, bracing keeps the structure stable, and each column stands on a footing below the floor slab. The clear span and eave height shown here are among the first dimensions to settle, because they follow directly from how the building will be used.

## Start with how the warehouse will be used

Everything else follows from use. Before any drawings are made, write down:

- **What will be stored or processed,** and how heavy and bulky it is
- **How goods move,** by hand, pallet trolley, forklift or conveyor
- **How vehicles arrive,** including the largest truck or container that must enter
- **How much space is needed now and later,** including room for expansion
- **Any special needs,** such as temperature control, ventilation, dust control or fire protection

A building planned around real operations works better than one designed only by floor area.

## Choose and assess the site

The site affects both design and cost. Look at:

1. **Size and shape:** enough room for the building, truck movement, loading areas and parking
2. **Access:** road width and turning space for the heaviest vehicles
3. **Ground conditions:** soil bearing capacity and groundwater affect the foundations and floor
4. **Levels and drainage:** how rainwater will drain away from the building and yard
5. **Utilities:** power, water and drainage availability and capacity
6. **Approvals and regulations:** requirements of the relevant authority, which should be checked directly before design starts

## Decide on the structural system

Warehouses are generally built with one of a few structural approaches, and the right one depends on span, height, loads, budget and speed.

| Approach | Typical features |
| -------- | ---------------- |
| Reinforced concrete frame | Columns, beams and slabs cast on site, suitable for heavier loads and multiple levels |
| Steel frame with sheeted roof and walls | Long clear spans and faster erection for single-level buildings |
| Mixed systems | Concrete for floors and lower walls, steel for roof framing |

Key questions are the clear span you need between columns, the clear height under the roof, the loads on the floor and roof, and how much of the building may need to be extended later. ## Plan the floor carefully

The floor is the hardest-working part of a warehouse. It carries stored goods and moving vehicles and is expensive to repair once in use. Consider:

- **Load:** the weight of stacked goods, racking and the heaviest vehicles
- **Surface:** flat, level and durable enough for the equipment that will run over it
- **Joints and cracks:** how concrete will be jointed and cured to control cracking
- **Slope and drainage:** gentle falls where water must drain
- **Sub-base:** compacted, well-prepared layers under the slab

Poor sub-base preparation is a common cause of floor settlement and cracking, so it deserves proper supervision.

## Think about doors, docks and circulation

How goods enter and leave the building shapes the layout:

- Door widths and heights should suit the largest vehicles and loads
- Loading docks or ramps should suit the vehicles that will use them
- Internal aisles must be wide enough for the handling equipment
- Separating people from vehicle routes improves safety

A rough traffic plan drawn early often reveals problems that are easy to fix on paper and expensive to fix on site.

## Light, ventilation, fire and safety

Warehouses need more than a roof and walls:

- **Natural light and ventilation** reduce energy use and improve conditions
- **Fire safety** requirements depend on the stored material and the building rules that apply, so confirm them with the relevant authority
- **Electrical layout** should allow for lighting, equipment and future loads
- **Safe working conditions** include clear exits, signage and protection from falls and moving equipment

## Prepare the budget and schedule

Large simple buildings can be built quickly, but early cost planning is still essential. A sound budget separates:

1. Earthwork and site preparation
2. Foundations and structure
3. Floor slab and external paving
4. Roof and wall cladding or masonry
5. Doors, docks and openings
6. Services such as electrical, plumbing and drainage
7. Design fees, approvals and contingency

## A short planning checklist

- Define the use, loads and vehicles
- Confirm the site, access and approvals
- Carry out a soil investigation
- Choose the structural system and clear span
- Design the floor and sub-base carefully
- Plan doors, docks and circulation
- Allow for light, ventilation, fire safety and services
- Prepare a written scope, estimate and schedule
